Working Papers

### Highway Access, Firm Dynamics, and Urban Shadows
*With Mark Schelker*
<!-- [PDF](/files/Schelker_Schmutz_Highways.pdf) -->

We examine how the staggered construction of Switzerland's highways affected firms in non-urban municipalities. We digitize the universe of Swiss corporations, build a four-decade municipal panel of firm counts, creations, closures, and relocations, and exploit the timing of highway-access openings to estimate the effect of proximity to highway access on firms at the municipal level. Highway access durably and gradually increases the number of firms. Firm entries drive the effect by exceeding exits, and we find no conclusive results on relocation of existing firms: highways generate net local growth rather than spatial reorganization. The response concentrates among smaller firms, which exhibit higher turnover. The effects are far from being spatially uniform. Exits rise regardless of proximity to urban centers, while the urban shadows deter entries near large urban centers. The results show that proximity to transport infrastructure reshapes not only the number of firms in non-urban municipalities, but also their composition and spatial distribution.

### Place-Based Policy and Firm Dynamics: Evidence from Switzerland
<!-- [PDF](/files/Schmutz_PlaceBased.pdf) -->

This paper evaluates the long-run effects of the Lex Bonny, a Swiss place-based industrial policy introduced in the late 1970s to support distressed municipalities through investment incentives and tax relief. Using newly digitized administrative records on the universe of Swiss joint-stock companies, I build a municipal panel from the 1960s to 1993 and study how eligibility affected firm stocks and flows (entry, exit, relocation). I exploit geographic variation in eligibility in a dynamic difference-in-differences design and use distance-based specifications to test for displacement. I find a gradual, persistent increase in the number of firms in eligible municipalities, driven mainly by higher entry. Spatial estimates suggest effects are localized, with limited spillovers to nearby areas. Complementary analyses of workers and taxpayers point to modest and heterogeneous responses: firm growth does not translate into employment gains, and changes in the tax base are concentrated in lower-to-middle brackets. Overall, Lex Bonny increased firm density in targeted areas, with no effects on local labor-market and fiscal outcomes in eligible municipalities.

### Firms, Directors, and the Geography of Corporate Networks: Evidence from Swiss Firms, 1960–2000
*With Mark Schelker*
<!-- [PDF](/files/Schelker_Schmutz_Networks.pdf) -->

This paper documents how distance and internal borders shape the geography of Swiss corporate networks over the second half of the twentieth century. Using comprehensive data on all Swiss corporations and their boards of directors, we reconstruct the bipartite firm–director network and its firm-to-firm and director-to-director projections. We aggregate the three different networks to the municipal level. We combine descriptive evidence with gravity-style dyadic regressions that model the intensity of connections between pairs of municipalities as a function of distance and shared institutional or cultural boundaries (e.g., cantons, language regions, and religions). Our results suggest large and persistent effects of distances and borders over time. In comparison, improvements in transport accessibility do not seem to play a major role in municipal-level economic interactions.
